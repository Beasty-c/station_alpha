# A Victorian Mansion and its Estate

A procedurally modelled Queen Anne / Second Empire mansion of about 1885 and
the park it stands in, built with Blender's Python API and rendered in
Cycles. It runs unchanged on `bpy` 4.2 and 5.0. There are no external assets: every profile, moulding,
material and tree is generated by the code in this repository, so the whole
model is reproducible from a single command.

```
pip install bpy==5.0.1                     # or 4.2.0
python3 scripts/build_blend.py             # writes victorian_estate.blend
python3 scripts/render_gallery.py preview  # daylight views into renders/
python3 scripts/render_dusk.py preview     # the same house with its lamps lit
```

## What is modelled

**The house** is three rectangular masses — a main block, a service wing
behind it, a gabled pavilion on the east — plus an engaged round tower at the
south-west corner.

- Rusticated stone plinth, water table, belt courses at each floor line, wide
  corner boards, and a full entablature: frieze, dentil course, sawn scroll
  brackets and a moulded cornice.
- 60-odd double-hung sash windows, each with stiles, rails and muntins in two
  sashes, a boxed frame, a weathered sill over a moulded apron, a casing with
  rosette corner blocks, and a hood on console brackets. Flat, segmental,
  round and pointed heads; louvred shutters; leaded and stained glazing.
- A two-storey canted bay with turned colonettes and a balustraded balcony,
  and a smaller hipped bay on the pavilion.
- A porte-cochere on the east front: paired posts on stone piers, sawn
  spandrels, a bracketed entablature and a balustraded flat over it, with the
  drive running through.
- Cast hopper heads and downpipes with collars, ears and swan-neck shoes.
- Attic lights in the gable fields, snow guards above the mansard eaves, and
  openings that differ from one another - a fifth of the lower sashes stand
  partly raised, a quarter carry a roller blind half drawn.
- The entrance: panelled double leaves with an etched upper light, stained
  sidelights, a fanlight transom and a bracketed hood.
- A Second Empire mansard slated slate by slate, with patterned band courses,
  four dormers a side, cast-iron ridge cresting and corbelled chimney stacks.
- Gable roofs with sawn bargeboards, fish-scale shingle gable fields and apex
  finials.
- A wrap-around veranda: deck boards on a lattice-screened skirt, turned posts
  on plinth blocks, a turned balustrade, spindle friezes and sawn spandrels in
  every bay head, a beam and cornice, a slated shed roof, and stone steps with
  newels.
- The tower: one revolve carries the plinth, both string courses and the
  corbelled cornice; then a slated candle-snuffer spire, a copper collar, a
  finial and a weather vane.

**The estate** sits on an analytic height field — rolling parkland flattened
into a level terrace around the house.

- A curving carriage approach from the gates, the sweep round a tiered
  fountain, a service branch to the stables, and flagged garden walks.
- A raised stone terrace with a turned balustrade, piers and urns.
- A formal box parterre: four hedged quarters on gravel walks, topiary at the
  centres and corners, flower beds, and a sundial at the crossing.
- Brick perimeter wall with piers and moulded coping, stone gate piers with
  urns, and wrought-iron carriage gates.
- Carriage house with arched doors, a hay loft and a louvred cupola, on a
  cobbled yard with a dovecote and a pump over its trough.
- An iron-and-glass conservatory, planted: a tiled walk, iron staging, pots
  and tub palms, because the point of a glasshouse is that you see into it.
- An octagonal gazebo, a gate lodge, a lake, and light iron park railings.
- A walled kitchen garden with cross walks, rotation beds, cold frames and a
  potting shed, and an orchard planted as a quincunx.
- Cast-iron gas lamp standards, park benches, urns on plinths and statuary.
- About 140 trees of ten species, clipped hedges, topiary, beds, and ivy on
  the service elevations.

1.95 million vertices in the scene, of which 1.12 million are actually
stored: the park's trees are linked duplicates of a dozen prototypes rather
than a joined mesh, which Cycles instances natively.

## How it is put together

```
victorian_estate/
  core/
    config.py      dimensions, storey heights, block massing, palette
    meshkit.py     lathe, mitred sweep, prisms, booleans, instancing
    ornament.py    the Victorian parts catalogue
    materials.py   34 texture-free procedural PBR materials
    render.py      sky, sun, architectural camera, render presets
  mansion/         shell, windows, bay, entrance, roof, veranda, tower
  grounds/         terrain, hardscape, planting, layout, outbuildings
  build.py         orchestrates the whole model
scripts/           build, render, and the two verification tools
```

Two ideas carry most of the weight.

**Everything is a lathe or a sweep.** Victorian architecture repeats a small
catalogue of shapes at every scale: a turned post is a baluster is a colonette
is a finial, and a cyma recta is a crown mould is a sill is a plinth cap.
`ornament.py` holds that catalogue as 2D profiles, and `meshkit.lathe` and
`meshkit.sweep` turn them into geometry. The sweep mitres its corners, which
is what stops a cornice tearing open where it turns.

**Composition is data, tessellation is code.** Which windows go where on a
facade is a design decision, so it is written out: an `Elevation` knows where
a wall face is and which way it looks, and a `Bay` says what stands at a given
offset and on which floors. Cutting the opening, building the joinery and
orienting it is then automatic. The veranda works the same way, from a matched
pair of plan polylines, and so does the estate layout, from exclusion zones
and placement rules rather than a uniform scatter.

## Verification

Modelling without a viewport means faults are invisible until they show up in
a render, so two scripts check the things that go wrong:

```
python3 scripts/check_profiles.py      # every moulding profile
python3 scripts/audit_geometry.py      # every mesh in the built model
python3 scripts/check_reproducible.py  # the same model twice
python3 scripts/elevations.py          # the four elevations, in parallel
```

`check_profiles.py` checks each profile is closed, wound counter-clockwise,
free of duplicate points, and non-self-intersecting — a profile wound the
wrong way sweeps a solid with inverted normals, and a duplicated point
pinches the swept surface.

`audit_geometry.py` builds the whole estate and reports inverted normals
(a closed mesh with negative signed volume), degenerate faces, non-manifold
edges and loose vertices.

`check_cameras.py` fires a grid of rays through each view and reports what
fraction of the frame is filled by geometry close to the camera. A camera has
no collision, so a view set up inside a tree canopy renders as a wall of
leaves with nothing to warn you but the image, half an hour later.

`check_reproducible.py` builds the estate twice and compares object names and
vertex counts. Seeding a tree from `hash(species)` looks harmless, but Python
randomises string hashing per process, so the park was growing differently on
every run. All three exit non-zero on failure.

Between them they caught most of the real bugs in this project: linked
instances collapsing onto the origin because `matrix_world` is stale until the
depsgraph evaluates; `lathe` and `sweep` winding their faces inward; boolean
cutters silently no-oping because a clockwise outline extruded inside out;
cusped foils tearing open where lobe arcs overlapped; gable roof quads twisted
because ridge points were listed in the opposite order to eave points; and
ground surfacing z-fighting to black wherever two paths crossed at the same
height.

The rest came from looking at renders, which is the only way to catch the
faults that are geometrically valid but architecturally wrong: eaves brackets
lying flat against the wall instead of standing edge-on to it, bargeboards
rotated up off the ridge instead of down the rake, a veranda roof cutting
across the heads of the windows behind it, an attic storey whose windows did
not fit under their own cornice, ivy climbing thin air beside the house
rather than up it, and - the one that took an orthographic elevation to see -
the wing and pavilion measuring their bay offsets from the world origin
instead of from their own centres, which put every one of their openings off
its wall.
